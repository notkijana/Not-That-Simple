# What Is Autism / ASD?

*Autism Encyclopedia — Chapter 01 · An introduction*

> **The short version:** Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition. It affects how a person experiences communication, social interaction, routines, interests, and sensory information. It is not one personality type, and it does not look the same in everyone. [1](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder)

## 1. What does “autism spectrum” mean?

**Autism** and **ASD** refer to the same diagnosis. *Neurodevelopmental* means the brain develops and processes information differently, beginning early in life.

The word **spectrum** does **not** mean everyone falls on one line from “a little autistic” to “very autistic.” Think of a sound mixer with separate sliders for communication, sensory sensitivity, flexibility, and support needs. Each person's settings can be different. Their experience can also vary with the environment, stress, and available support. [1](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder) [3](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/1/e20193447/36917/Identification-Evaluation-and-Management-of)

**Example:** Someone may confidently lead a work meeting but struggle to process an emotionally intense conversation at home. Being capable in one situation doesn't make the struggle in another situation less real.

## 2. What are the main characteristics?

Clinicians look for differences in **both** of these areas: [2](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)

### A. Social communication and interaction

This can involve:

- Understanding tone, facial expressions, hints, sarcasm, or unspoken expectations.
- Knowing when to speak, what response is expected, or how to handle back-and-forth conversation.
- Communicating feelings in ways other people recognize.

**Example:** “Do whatever you want” might be understood literally, even when the speaker actually expects the person to recognize that they're upset. The misunderstanding doesn't necessarily mean a lack of care.

### B. Repetitive patterns, focused interests, and sensory differences

This can involve:

- Preferring predictability and finding sudden changes difficult.
- Developing intense, detailed interests or focusing deeply on a topic.
- Repeating movements or sounds (*stimming*), sometimes to regulate feelings or sensory input.
- Experiencing sounds, lights, textures, or other sensations much more—or much less—intensely than others.

**Example:** An unexpected change to a plan may seem minor to one person but require significant mental effort for an autistic person to adjust to. It's not necessarily about refusing to compromise. [2](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/index.html) [4](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/signs-in-adults/)

## 3. The three support levels

A diagnosis may include a level describing how much support someone needs in **social communication** and **restricted or repetitive behaviors**. [2](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/index.html) [3](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/1/e20193447/36917/Identification-Evaluation-and-Management-of)

| Level | Clinical description | In everyday terms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Requires support | May manage many activities independently but have significant difficulty with social demands, transitions, or organization without support. |
| **2** | Requires substantial support | More noticeable communication differences and greater difficulty with changes or daily demands, even when some support is available. |
| **3** | Requires very substantial support | Major communication and flexibility challenges that can affect many aspects of daily life and require extensive support. |

**Important:** Levels describe *support needs*, not intelligence, character, worth, or capacity for love. Needs can differ across settings and over time; a level is not a perfect summary of a person. [3](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/145/1/e20193447/36917/Identification-Evaluation-and-Management-of)

## 4. What can autism look like day to day?

| Situation | What might be happening internally |
| --- | --- |
| **A complicated conversation** | The person is still processing the first point while more information keeps arriving. |
| **A crowded restaurant** | Background music, voices, smells, and conversation compete for attention until it becomes difficult to think. |
| **An unexpected schedule change** | The person needs time to mentally rebuild the plan they had prepared for. |
| **After a full day of socializing** | They may seem fine in public but feel exhausted once they get somewhere safe and quiet. |
| **A very direct response** | They may be prioritizing clear information rather than trying to sound cold or dismissive. |

**Anime comparison:** Imagine Killua rapidly explaining something complicated to Gon in *Hunter × Hunter*, and Gon's brain figuratively overheating until he can't keep up. That is one way to *picture* the mental overload some autistic people experience when too much information arrives at once. **It is an analogy, not an autism diagnosis of Gon, and not something every autistic person experiences.**

### Shutdowns, meltdowns, and masking

- **Shutdown:** Overwhelm may make someone go quiet, withdraw, or struggle to respond.
- **Meltdown:** Overwhelm may instead appear outwardly, through crying, yelling, pacing, or visible distress. These reactions are not necessarily deliberate.
- **Masking:** Someone may consciously or unconsciously hide autistic traits or copy expected social behavior. They can appear comfortable while using substantial energy to get through the interaction. [4](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/signs-in-adults/) [5](https://autismsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/AutismSociety_Autistic-Meltdowns-Shutdowns_2025-06V2F_Digital.pdf)

## 5. How is autism diagnosed?

There is **no blood test or brain scan that independently diagnoses autism**. A qualified clinician evaluates a person's developmental history, patterns of behavior, communication, and everyday functioning. Adults can be diagnosed, even if they learned to hide or work around their differences for years. [1](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder) [6](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/diagnosis/index.html)

Under the **DSM-5-TR** diagnostic framework, clinicians assess:

1. Differences in **all three** social-communication areas: social reciprocity, nonverbal communication, and relationships.
2. At least **two of four** types of restricted/repetitive features: repetitive behavior or speech, insistence on sameness, focused interests, and sensory differences.
3. Evidence that the characteristics began during early development, affect important parts of life, and are not better explained by another condition alone. [2](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/hcp/diagnosis/index.html)

An adult evaluation may involve interviews, questionnaires, clinical observation, and questions about childhood. An online screening quiz can suggest whether further evaluation makes sense, but **it cannot establish a diagnosis by itself**. [1](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder)

## 6. What autism does **not** automatically mean

- **Not a lack of empathy or love.** Understanding or expressing emotions may look different from what others expect.
- **Not low intelligence.** Autistic people have a wide range of intellectual abilities.
- **Not always obvious.** Someone can have a job, friendships, and a relationship while still experiencing meaningful difficulties.
- **Not a childhood-only condition.** Autism begins early in development and continues into adulthood. [1](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder) [4](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/signs-in-adults/)

## 7. The main thing to remember in a relationship

**Don't judge an internal experience only by how it looks from the outside.** Silence may mean processing, directness may mean an attempt at clarity, and needing quiet may mean overload—not rejection.

A helpful starting point is to **communicate expectations clearly, allow processing time, give advance notice of changes when possible, and ask what support actually helps**. These are starting points, not rules that apply to every autistic person.
